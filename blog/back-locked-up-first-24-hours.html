Back Locked Up? Here's What to Do in the First 24 Hours

Published on May 20, 2026

It happens in an instant. You bend over to pick up a sock, twist to grab your coffee, or just stand up from your desk—and suddenly, you can’t move. Your lower back is completely locked up.

I’ve had patients describe this exact moment to me hundreds of times. They usually say it feels like a vice grip clamped down on their spine, and even taking a deep breath is agonizing. If you’re reading this while stuck on the floor or frozen in a chair, don't panic. Here is what is actually going on and exactly what you need to do over the next 24 hours.

What's Actually Happening

When your back locks up, it’s rarely a bone slipping completely out of place, even though it feels that way. Most of the time, it's a severe muscle spasm. Your body senses a threat—like a joint moving incorrectly or a disc getting irritated—and your muscles slam the brakes. They freeze the area to prevent further damage. It’s a protective mechanism. A very painful, frustrating protective mechanism.

What to Do in the First 24 Hours

1. Stop and find a neutral position

Don't try to push through it. Find a position where the pain decreases. For a lot of people, that’s lying on their back with their knees bent and feet flat on the floor, or resting their lower legs up on a chair. This takes the pressure off your lower back.

2. Ice, not heat

I know a hot shower sounds amazing right now. But heat brings more inflammation to an area that is already swollen and angry. Grab an ice pack, wrap it in a thin towel, and apply it to the painful area for 15 to 20 minutes at a time. Do this every hour or two.

3. Keep moving (gently)

You might want to stay in bed for two days. Don't. Prolonged bed rest actually makes the stiffness worse. Try to get up and walk around the house for a few minutes every hour. Just simple, gentle movement. If it causes sharp, shooting pain, stop immediately. But a little bit of dull soreness during movement is okay.

4. Breathe

When we are in pain, we take shallow breaths and tense up. This feeds the muscle spasm. Focus on taking slow, deep belly breaths to help signal to your nervous system that it’s safe to relax.

When It's Time to Get Checked

Most acute spasms will start to calm down within 24 to 48 hours if you manage them correctly. However, you need to get evaluated if:

  • The pain is shooting down your leg past your knee
  • You have numbness or tingling in your legs or feet
  • You experience weakness in your legs
  • The intense pain hasn't started to improve after a couple of days

Important Note: Even if the spasm starts to calm down, getting an evaluation is a smart move. Often, an underlying joint issue is what caused the muscles to lock up in the first place, and addressing the underlying issue is necessary to help prevent another flare-up.
